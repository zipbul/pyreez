PostgreSQL is a strong default for many MVPs, but it is not automatically the right one. It produces strictly worse outcomes than MongoDB for this 4-person, 6-month team under three specific, distinguishable conditions. Each is independently sufficient — any one firing is enough to make Postgres the worse choice — and each is a property of the product's data and access patterns, not a generic preference. The three are ordered below by data-shape category, not by a settled severity ranking: the panel did not agree on which is most severe. Specifically, the severity of Condition 1 (live non-additive schema change) is itself contested — one analyst rated it the single most severe failure mode (critical), another rated it clearly below the other two (medium) on the grounds that its mitigations are cheap and standard. That disagreement is unresolved and is preserved in the body, not papered over. Numeric thresholds and prescriptions below (the ~one-third query share, the small-entity-set ER test, the short spike) are decision heuristics, not measured facts. Operational generalizations marked [judgment] below (e.g. that MVP-scale rewrites are often sub-second, that lock_timeout removes the lock cascade, that managed services bundle pooling and tuned autovacuum) are widely-held practitioner experience, not claims verified in this run; the load-bearing parts of the answer are the documented database-engine mechanisms, not these operational generalizations.

CONDITION 1 — Core objects are still being discovered in production and require frequent NON-ADDITIVE schema changes on hot, populated tables. (Severity contested: rated critical by one analyst, medium by another; see the carveout below.)
When core persisted objects keep changing in non-additive ways AFTER production traffic starts, PostgreSQL turns ordinary product iteration into lock-sensitive DDL. Additive changes (new tables, nullable columns, non-volatile defaults that apply only to future writes) are cheap and do NOT rewrite the table — so the generic "migrations are painful" framing is wrong and is explicitly NOT the failure mode. The failure mode is the non-additive class on live tables. Per the PostgreSQL ALTER TABLE docs these operations differ in cost and should not be lumped together: a column TYPE change "will normally cause the entire table and its indexes to be rewritten" (the docs say "normally" — some type changes are exempt, e.g. when binary coercion makes a rewrite unnecessary); adding a column with a VOLATILE default forces a full table rewrite (whereas a constant default does not); and adding NOT NULL or a validated CHECK constraint to an already-populated column ordinarily requires a full-table SCAN to verify existing rows (a scan, not necessarily a rewrite). All of these take an ACCESS EXCLUSIVE lock for their duration. Under that lock, an ill-timed long-running query makes subsequent reads and writes queue behind the DDL, turning a schema tweak into an application stall. For a four-person team without a dedicated database operator on a deadline, reliably avoiding this (lock_timeout discipline, scheduling windows, shadow-table patterns) is [judgment] a plausible stumble rather than a certainty. MongoDB ships the same iteration as an application-level shape change, validating only new or stabilized data.
Carveout — and the source of the severity disagreement: how much this actually hurts depends on facts the team controls. [judgment] At MVP table sizes a rewrite or scan is typically fast; `SET lock_timeout` is the standard one-line guard against the lock cascade; and PostgreSQL 18 (released 2025) lets NOT NULL be added as NOT VALID, removing the up-front full-table scan from that sub-case (the NOT NULL NOT VALID capability is a verifiable PG18 feature; the claim that this "largely neutralizes" the condition is an inference, not a measured result). One analyst concluded from these mitigations that the condition is only medium severity; another held it critical because a discovery-phase product hits non-additive changes often and a four-person team will not reliably apply the mitigations under deadline. Both are recorded; the disagreement is not resolved here. What is NOT contested: the condition bites hardest when tables grow large within the window, non-additive changes are frequent rather than occasional, AND the team adopts neither lock_timeout nor PostgreSQL 18. Where those mitigations are in place and changes are mostly additive, this axis is survivable on Postgres.

CONDITION 2 — The dominant unit of work is a self-contained nested aggregate that is read and written whole, with few cross-aggregate joins. (High severity.)
When the hot path is fetching and mutating one bounded nested object as a unit — onboarding state, workflow/template definitions, tenant configuration, generated manifests — MongoDB's document is the native primitive and gives single-document atomic writes. PostgreSQL forces a structural tax either way: normalize and pay multi-table reads/writes for every aggregate operation, or store it as JSONB and serialize every concurrent edit through a row-level lock on the whole row. For a small team optimizing for shipping speed, that tax is paid on the most frequent operation in the product.
Keep Postgres anyway only if the aggregate stays bounded AND cross-aggregate queries are rare. This advantage is real but narrows fast: as the share of queries needing cross-entity filtering rises (treat roughly a third within the 6 months as the tipping point), MongoDB's $lookup joins are weak and the advantage erodes; and aggregates that grow without bound eventually hit MongoDB's 16 MiB document limit, forcing the relational model back in by hand. So this condition favors MongoDB specifically for bounded, aggregate-scoped workloads.

CONDITION 3 — The core domain entities are intentionally polymorphic or tenant-variable, and that variation must be queried. (High-to-medium severity.)
When schema heterogeneity is a first-class product feature — customer-defined fields, integration-specific payloads that must be filtered/searched, or versioned objects whose field sets materially differ by tenant or workflow — PostgreSQL forces a losing choice. Either the team writes a relational migration for every new variant (unsustainable churn for four people on a deadline), or it pushes the variance into JSONB and thereby abandons the fixed-schema clarity that justified choosing Postgres in the first place — while still carrying relational machinery it no longer benefits from. MongoDB models this natively: optional fields coexist with a validated stable core (its schema validation enforces a rule only when the field is present), so variants ship as application-level shape changes with no DDL. PostgreSQL loses here because its default contract is a fixed column set; MongoDB's default contract is optional fields plus conditional rules, which is exactly the shape of the workload.
Keep Postgres anyway only if the variable portion is small, opaque, and never filtered or joined on (store-and-return blobs). The moment variable fields must be queried, this fires.

ADOPTION CONDITION (when PostgreSQL remains the correct default):
Keep PostgreSQL if all three of the following hold (these are decision heuristics, with the suggested counts as rules of thumb, not thresholds derived from measurement):
(1) Near-term schema evolution is mostly additive; any non-additive DDL (type changes, volatile defaults, NOT NULL/CHECK on populated columns) is on a managed PostgreSQL 18 service with lock_timeout set (this neutralizes Condition 1).
(2) The primary access pattern is relational — joins, constraints, and cross-entity aggregation — rather than whole-aggregate document retrieval; single-aggregate whole-object reads/writes are a minority of the workload (this neutralizes Condition 2).
(3) Your handful of core entities have stable, known schemas you can draw as a fixed-column ER diagram before month one — variant fields are a small minority of entities or are non-queryable blobs (this neutralizes Condition 3).
If any of (1)-(3) fails, the recommended check is a short spike (on the order of a couple of days) modeling both databases against your actual top-10 queries before committing. If all three hold, the PostgreSQL default is correct and switching to MongoDB would be the worse decision.

CONDITIONAL / DROPPED findings (and why they are not among the three): One worker rated serverless/FaaS connection exhaustion (many cold function instances overwhelming a default connection limit) as its top finding, and another rated frequent partial updates to large JSONB documents (MVCC full-row copy plus GIN-index rewrite, causing bloat) as high. These are real failure modes, but they are NOT primary here for two reasons: (a) they are conditional on facts the prompt does not give — a serverless deployment model, and large write-heavy documents — whereas the three conditions above turn only on the product's own data shape and access pattern; and (b) [judgment] the connection-exhaustion case is, in common managed-service deployments, addressed by the connection pooling those services typically bundle (so it fires mainly for self-hosted/bare-metal, which this team profile is not), and the MVCC-bloat case rests on benchmarks the quality pass flagged as configuration-asymmetric and unverified. The "managed services bundle pooling" point is widely-held practitioner experience, not a fact verified in this run; if the team's deployment is serverless or self-hosted, re-elevate the connection finding. They are surfaced here and folded into the adoption conditions rather than asserted as one of the three. Separately set aside and excluded: "operational bloat/autovacuum degrades the MVP within ~60 days" ([judgment] default autovacuum on a managed service is generally adequate at MVP-scale write rates — again practitioner experience, not measured here) and "a prior MongoDB codebase makes the switch costly" (excluded on logical grounds, not operational ones: it is symmetric — it applies identically in reverse and is therefore not a PostgreSQL-specific weakness). All worker-cited benchmark magnitudes (throughput/storage multipliers, downtime seconds, usage percentages) were treated as unverified and excluded as load-bearing facts; the answer rests on documented engine mechanisms — ACCESS EXCLUSIVE locks during table rewrites/scans, JSONB whole-row write locking, MongoDB single-document write atomicity, the 16 MiB document limit, and PostgreSQL 18's NOT NULL NOT VALID — not on the numbers.
