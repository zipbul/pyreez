## Final Consolidated Analysis — Monorepo Full-Suite CI for 40 Engineers

---

### 1. Queue Saturation and Superlinear Wait-Time Growth

- **severity**: critical
- **confidence**: HIGH
- **target**: proposal; also challenges Analyst A (Finding 1)
- **steelman**: A single full-suite run per PR gives maximum integration confidence and requires no dependency-graph tooling.
- **weakness**: At 40 engineers producing ~50 CI runs/day (PRs plus re-runs), a 45-minute suite on finite runners pushes utilization past the ~75% threshold where queuing wait times grow **superlinearly**, not linearly as Analyst A states. This distinction matters: linear growth is plannable; superlinear growth means small increases in load (a release week, an incident) cause disproportionate wait-time spikes. Analyst A's falsification test — "multiply suite duration by peak concurrent PR count" — is also too simple: it models a deterministic system, ignoring variance in arrival times and service times. Queuing theory (Kingman's formula) shows that the *coefficient of variation* of inter-arrival and service times is a dominant factor. Bursty PR arrivals (post-standup, pre-deadline) and variable test durations (flaky retries, cache misses) inflate the coefficient of variation, making real-world wait times 2–5× worse than the deterministic estimate. The correct model is M/G/c or at minimum accounts for variance, not just means.
- **evidence**: Kingman's formula: E[wait] ≈ (ρ / (1−ρ)) × (c²_a + c²_s) / 2 × E[service time], where ρ is utilization. At ρ = 0.8 with moderate variance (c² ≈ 1 for both arrival and service), expected wait equals the service time itself — i.e., a 45-minute wait on top of a 45-minute run. The superlinear term (ρ/(1−ρ)) means going from 80% to 90% utilization doubles the wait. Analyst A's linear claim undersells the severity.
- **falsification**: Simulate with actual PR arrival timestamps (from VCS history) and measured suite durations using an M/G/c queue model. If 95th-percentile wait time stays under 15 minutes, the queue concern is deferrable.

### 2. Flaky Tests as a Team-Wide Blocker (Severity Dispute with Analyst A)

- **severity**: critical
- **confidence**: HIGH
- **target**: proposal; challenges Analyst A's rating of this as medium
- **steelman**: A uniform gate prevents any team from ignoring test quality elsewhere in the codebase, creating shared ownership.
- **weakness**: Analyst A rates flaky-test risk as **medium**. This is underweighted given the math. With 5,000 tests and a conservative 0.1% per-test flake rate, the per-run probability of at least one flaky failure is 1 − 0.999^5000 ≈ 99.3%. At 0.05% (half the conservative rate), it is still 1 − 0.9995^5000 ≈ 91.8%. This means virtually every run hits a spurious red, and the system degrades into a "retry until green" workflow — which compounds the queue saturation problem from Finding 1 by doubling effective run volume. Furthermore, Analyst A's falsification ("record flake rate for two weeks; if extrapolated red-build probability stays below 5%") is insufficient because it measures the suite at *current* size; the proposal locks the team into running the *entire future* suite. Flake probability grows monotonically with test count, and monorepo test count grows monotonically with time. A two-week measurement at today's size provides no bound on next quarter's rate.
- **evidence**: The binomial model is exact: P(≥1 flake) = 1 − (1 − p)^n where p is per-test flake rate and n is test count. Winters et al. in "Software Engineering at Google" describe Google's flake rate at roughly 1.5% of test executions [unverified specific figure but the order of magnitude is recalled], requiring dedicated deflaking infrastructure. Without that infrastructure — which the proposal does not include — the full-suite strategy is untenable.
- **falsification**: Run the full suite 50 times with zero code changes. If >45 are green, current flake rate is below the critical threshold — but this must be repeated quarterly as the suite grows.

### 3. Unrelated Failures Create Cross-Team Blocking

- **severity**: high
- **confidence**: HIGH
- **target**: proposal
- **steelman**: Whole-suite validation surfaces unexpected cross-cutting regressions that scoped testing would miss.
- **weakness**: The hidden assumption is that when a test fails, the PR author can determine whether their change caused it. Without affected-test mapping, a frontend engineer seeing a failing database migration test has no efficient way to triage. This creates two failure modes: (a) engineers begin ignoring CI (red normalization), or (b) engineers are blocked waiting for an unrelated team to investigate — creating O(k) cross-team dependency surfaces where k is the number of sub-teams. With 40 engineers across 5–8 sub-teams, the probability of at least one team's breakage blocking all others on any given day is 1 − (1−p)^k. Even at a modest p = 0.1 per-team daily breakage probability, with k = 6 teams: 1 − 0.9^6 ≈ 47% daily probability of a cross-team block.
- **evidence**: Mechanism: full-suite execution with no ownership annotation means all failures are undifferentiated red. The lack of a `CODEOWNERS`-like mapping from tests to responsible teams makes triage a manual process. The standard industry mitigation (test ownership metadata, blame-based routing) is absent from the proposal.
- **falsification**: If the monorepo has a functioning test-ownership system that auto-assigns flaky/broken tests to the responsible team and allows other teams' PRs to proceed with a warning (not a block), the cross-team blocking concern is mitigated.

### 4. Merge-Race Staleness

- **severity**: high
- **confidence**: HIGH
- **target**: proposal
- **steelman**: Testing every PR against the latest base ensures freshness.
- **weakness**: With ~25 merges/day during an 8-hour window, the mean interval between merges is ~19 minutes. A 45-minute CI run is stale by the time it completes; `main` has moved ~2.4 merges ahead on average. Requiring re-test against latest `main` before merge creates a cycle: rebase → re-run → main moves again. This is the "merge race" and is mathematically unwinnable when T_ci > T_merge unless a merge queue batches and serializes. But a merge queue with 45-minute batches throttles merge throughput to ~10–11 merges/day (one per batch), far below the 25/day demand — creating either a growing backlog or forcing engineers to wait until next day.
- **evidence**: If the merge queue tests batches of B PRs together, throughput is (B × merges) / T_ci per hour. To hit 25/day in 8 hours, you need B ≈ 3.1 per batch with 45-min T_ci — meaning each batch tests 3+ PRs simultaneously, and if any fails, the batch is bisected and re-run, cutting effective throughput further.
- **falsification**: If T_ci < T_merge (i.e., full suite under 15 minutes at current merge rate), the staleness problem does not arise. Measure both.

### 5. CI Cost Scaling Without Efficiency Gain

- **severity**: high
- **confidence**: HIGH
- **target**: proposal; reinforces Analyst A (Finding 2)
- **steelman**: A uniform pipeline eliminates the maintenance burden of per-package CI configuration.
- **weakness**: Analyst A correctly identifies that wasted work "approaches 1" as packages grow, but understates the hidden assumption: Analyst A's own falsification ("instrument one week of PRs to record minimal test set") actually produces the dependency graph needed for selective execution — meaning the falsification test, if it confirms the waste, also provides the solution. The deeper issue is that the proposal treats CI cost as fixed overhead rather than a variable correlated to risk. A one-line documentation fix and a core API change consume identical resources. At 50 runs/day × $0.50–$2.00/run (cloud CI with 45-min 8-core machines), annual cost is $9,000–$36,000 — meaningful but not fatal. The real cost is *opportunity cost*: those runner-hours cannot serve other CI needs (deploy pipelines, nightly builds, security scans) and the team is locked into scaling runners linearly with headcount.
- **evidence**: Cloud CI pricing (GitHub Actions, CircleCI, BuildKite) charges per compute-minute. A 45-minute run on an 8-core Linux runner costs roughly $0.50–$1.50 depending on provider [unverified exact pricing, order of magnitude from publicly listed rates]. 50 runs/day × 365 × $1.00 = $18,250/year — growing linearly with team size.
- **falsification**: If the team uses self-hosted runners with spare capacity and the full suite runs in under 10 minutes, compute cost is negligible and this concern drops to low.

### 6. Retrofit Cost of Selective Execution

- **severity**: high
- **confidence**: MEDIUM
- **target**: proposal
- **steelman**: Starting simple (full suite) avoids premature optimization; the team can adopt selective execution when pain justifies it.
- **weakness**: The hidden assumption is that migrating to selective execution is an incremental improvement atop the existing setup. In practice, selective execution requires accurate, enforced module boundaries — specifically, a build system that tracks dependencies at the target level (Bazel, Buck, Nx, Pants). If the monorepo is built with `make`, `npm scripts`, or language-native tools (cargo workspaces without explicit inter-crate visibility control, or a monolithic `go.mod`), implicit dependencies accumulate: shared test fixtures, file-system side effects, import-by-path across module boundaries. Retrofitting explicit boundaries into a codebase that has grown for 12+ months without them is a multi-month project (often called "modularization" or "build migration"), during which both old and new build systems must coexist. The "optimize later" escape hatch may cost more than doing it right initially.
- **evidence**: Mechanism: without enforced boundaries, engineers naturally take the shortest import path. Over N months, the number of undeclared cross-module dependencies grows roughly as O(N × engineers). Each undeclared dependency must be discovered, declared, or refactored during migration. This is the "hidden coupling" problem well-documented in modularization literature.
- **falsification**: If the team adopts Bazel, Nx, or equivalent with enforced visibility rules from day one (even while running full suite), the retrofit cost is near-zero because the dependency graph is already accurate.

### 7. Developer Behavior Degradation

- **severity**: medium
- **confidence**: MEDIUM
- **target**: proposal
- **steelman**: A strict, uniform CI gate prevents undisciplined merges and ensures collective code ownership.
- **weakness**: When CI feedback exceeds ~10–15 minutes, engineers context-switch, lose flow, and adapt in counterproductive ways: batching more changes per PR (increasing review burden and defect rate), stacking dependent PRs (creating merge-order nightmares), or seeking workarounds (direct pushes, skipping tests). These behaviors are individually rational but collectively undermine the quality guarantee the full-suite strategy was meant to provide. The hidden assumption is that engineers will tolerate arbitrary wait times without behavioral adaptation.
- **evidence**: DORA / State of DevOps research identifies CI/CD lead time as a key predictor of software delivery performance, with elite teams targeting sub-10-minute feedback. The mechanism is cognitive: long waits break the tight feedback loop needed for incremental development, incentivizing larger batch sizes, which correlate with higher failure rates.
- **falsification**: After 3 months, measure (a) average PR size in lines of code, (b) proportion of PRs merged with CI overridden or bypassed, and (c) engineer satisfaction with CI via survey. If PR size hasn't increased, bypass rate is <2%, and satisfaction is >70%, this concern is not materializing.

### Challenge to Analyst A's Acceptability Condition

Analyst A's condition — "the full test suite has been measured to finish in under ten minutes on production CI hardware at peak concurrency **and an impact-analysis step has been shown to reduce executed tests by at least 70%**" — is internally contradictory. If an impact-analysis step is added that selects only affected tests, the team is **no longer running the full test suite on every PR**. Analyst A's acceptable-if condition is effectively "the proposal is acceptable if you abandon the proposal." This is analytically correct (the proposal as stated is likely unworkable) but should be stated plainly as a rejection rather than framed as an acceptance condition.

---

### Consolidated Acceptability Condition

This proposal is acceptable only if **all** of the following hold:

1. The full test suite completes in **under 10 minutes** on production CI hardware, including projected 12-month test growth.
2. Enough parallel CI runners are provisioned to keep queue utilization **below 60%** at peak daily load (not average).
3. A **flaky-test quarantine mechanism** (automatic detection, isolation, and ownership assignment) is operational from day one.
4. A **merge queue** with batch-testing capability is in place to prevent merge-race staleness.
5. The build system enforces **explicit module boundaries** from the start (enabling future migration to selective execution without a retrofit project).
6. The team commits to a **trigger threshold** (e.g., suite duration exceeding 15 minutes or queue P95 wait exceeding 10 minutes) at which selective execution will be adopted — with the tooling foundation from condition 5 already in place.

If any of these conditions is unmet, the proposal should be revised to include affected-target analysis before adoption.
